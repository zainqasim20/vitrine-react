## PART 0 — METHODOLOGY & HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT

Every claim in this document traces back to a real observation: a real screenshot, real page text, or a real audience comment, captured by directly browsing 212 live Behance projects (not synthesized, not guessed). Where a finding was confirmed independently 2+ times across unrelated projects, it is called a **pattern**. Where it was observed once but is a striking, high-value technique, it is called a **technique** and flagged as a single data point. Where established design theory fills a gap the research didn't directly touch, it is labeled **[theory]**.

This document answers: *what must exist, what should exist, and what separates a forgettable project from a trusted one.* Its companion document, `Presentation-Styles-v3.md`, answers a different question: *how do you visually construct any given frame with genuine creative craft?* Use both together — this document tells you what to include; the other tells you how to stage it.

## PART 2 — COVER & HERO CONSTRUCTION: THE REAL PATTERNS

### 2.1 Two legitimate cover philosophies for self-promotional (portfolio) covers — not one

The earlier synthesis assumed minimal/confident typography was the safe default. Real data contradicts this as a hard rule.

- **Path A — Minimal/Confident**: oversized display type, restrained palette, editorial spacing. Real examples span 100K–655K views.
- **Path B — Maximalist/Playful**: marquee/ticker-tape scrolling banners, cartoon mascots, humor-driven personality copy ("Born to create," "Raised by deadlines"). The single highest-performing personal portfolio in the entire 212-link sample (199.1K+ views) used this path, not Path A.

**Rule: choose based on the designer's actual personality and target industry — do not default to minimal as automatically "safer."** The data does not support that assumption.

### 2.2 Client-project cover conventions (distinct from self-portfolio covers)

- **Meta-info header bar**: role / year / project-type / owner laid out left-to-right at the very top. Confirmed independently dozens of times, across landing pages, mobile apps, and dashboards. This is the single most standardized real convention found in the entire research set.
- **Oversized wordmark overlay**: the project or brand name set enormous, running directly behind or through the hero mockup — type and product physically overlapping. Confirmed in at least four visually distinct executions:
  - Clean geometric sans-serif on a bold flat color (confirmed on a 360K-view mobile app, using bright yellow — not the "safe" dark/neutral default)
  - Graffiti/street-art brush lettering with a real photographed hand holding the device (176.6K-view range)
  - 3D-extruded wobbly lettering (character/3D category)
  - Faint gray watermark-scale type as a background texture rather than a foreground statement (real estate/fintech dashboard covers)
- **Photographic-backdrop logo reveal**: logo/wordmark overlaid directly on a real photographic scene, not a flat brand-color card. Confirmed on the single highest-performing brand identity project in the entire sample (176.6K+ views, a travel brand revealed over a bridge/mountain photograph). **This means the "flat color reveal" rule from earlier documentation is not universal — it's a strong default, with a real, validated photographic alternative.**
- **Annotated hero**: real stat callouts and labeled component annotations placed directly on the hero image itself (not deferred to a later detail frame). Confirmed on a real dental-implant landing page; drew a direct audience comment praising the "instant trust" this built.
- **Dark atmospheric glass staging**: device mockup on a dark, blurred, glassmorphic background rather than a flat card or literal desk photograph — a real third option distinct from the flat-vs-photographic binary.
- **Movie-credits opening**: bold wordmark + tagline, then a literal cast/role list — confirmed on a packaging project that personified each product flavor as a named character, and independently on a 3D character-universe project with a pure typographic title card before any art appears.

## PART 3 — STRUCTURAL & NARRATIVE CONVENTIONS

### 3.1 Labeled section structures — real, not assumed

Confirmed independently in at least five distinct real phrasings:
- `THE CHALLENGE` / `THE CONCEPT` / `LOGO CONCEPT` / `Visual Language` / `POSTER SYSTEM`
- `Foreword` / `Brand Naming` / `Logo Type` / `Brand identity system` / `Target` / `House Style` / `Content Communication`
- `Goal` / `Research` / `Result` / `Why [X] fits [Brand]` / `How it was reinterpreted` / `Type pairing` / `Behaviour and scale`
- `01 — The Idea` / `02 — The Identity` / `03 — The Experience`
- `About the client` / `Objective` / `Solution` / `Result`

**Rule: a real case study almost always has explicit section labels, not just visual pacing.** The Narrate stage should generate labeled section headers, not just flowing paragraph text.

### 3.2 The "Target" / dual-audience framing technique

One real, sophisticated case study explicitly separated its communication strategy: *"speaks two languages — B2B trust and credibility, B2C sensory and emotional connection."* This is a real, valuable rhetorical device for any project serving two different stakeholder types (e.g., internal tools that also need executive buy-in).

### 3.3 Real "category-cliché avoidance" statements

Confirmed at least three separate times, in different categories:
- A coastal bar identity: avoided "usual beach-bar clichés"
- A healthy-snack brand: deliberately broke away from "earthy tones and rustic visuals typical of healthy snack brands"
- A B2B fintech mascot: built specifically to escape "a category full of safe, forgettable B2B fintech design"

**Rule: naming and rejecting the obvious/expected treatment for the category is a real, repeated, high-value rhetorical technique** — it signals the designer understood the category deeply enough to consciously choose against it, not that they simply didn't know the convention.

### 3.4 Process transparency — showing real rejected options, not just the final choice

Confirmed technique: presenting 2–3 real alternate directions (e.g., two full colorway options, labeled) before revealing the final choice. This is distinct from a craft-progression Process Timeline — it's specifically about showing genuine decision-making, which drew direct audience appreciation ("makes the process feel as considered as the final outcome").

### 3.5 Real audience-facing critical questions to design for

Real comments repeatedly asked designers direct process questions in public comments:
- "Did the final result evolve a lot from your initial sketches?"
- "Was clarity at small print sizes part of the first brief or a later refinement?"
- "Is this a custom typeface or heavily modified?"

**Implication: audiences actively probe for genuine process depth.** A case study that can answer these questions preemptively (via a process section) reads as more credible than one that only shows the final result.

## PART 4 — CREDIT & TOOL DISCLOSURE (exhaustive, real)

### 4.1 Role-based credit granularity — real, confirmed extremely detailed

Real projects credit not just "design" and "photography" but: Creative Director, Art Director, Project Manager, Brand Strategist, Copywriter, 3D & Mockup (as its own distinct role, separate from graphic design), Motion Designer (credited even on static branding projects — implying an animated component exists), Sound Design (credited even on a primarily-visual Behance platform), Interactive/Generator Development (for projects with a coded interactive element), and even the physical print vendor by name.

### 4.2 AI tools are now a normalized, undifferentiated part of the Tools field

Confirmed repeatedly, across categories: ChatGPT, Midjourney, Firefly, Claude Design, Lovable, Kling AI — all listed in the same Tools field as Figma/Photoshop/Illustrator, with **no separate disclosure section required by convention.** This is the current real norm, not a controversial edge case.

**However**, two real projects went further with an explicit, detailed AI-workflow disclosure section:
- A real, credited commercial Acer motion graphics project included a dedicated "AI R&D" section explaining precisely how AI-generated abstract visual assets were combined with 3D product renders, and that AI was used to help evolve camera motion — with explicit acknowledgment that human curation and integration were still required to meet visual standards.
- A government-commissioned short film about child labor disclosed that its 3D animation, music composition, and voice dubbing were AI-generated, paired with an explicit statement that rigorous human art direction remained essential given the sensitivity of the subject matter.

**Rule: standard AI tool use = list it in Tools, no special treatment needed. Substantial or sensitive AI use = a short dedicated disclosure paragraph explaining the human-AI division of labor, especially for emotionally or ethically weighty subject matter.**

### 4.3 Font and asset attribution

Real confirmed convention: crediting the specific typeface AND its foundry/designer by name (e.g., "DX Aiter Typeface by Dirtyline Studio") — not just listing "Illustrator" as if the typography were self-made.

### 4.4 Contact and business-development conventions

- Direct contact info (email, WhatsApp, Telegram) embedded in the project description text itself, separate from Behance's own "Hire" button — confirmed dozens of times.
- Explicit freelance pricing lists embedded directly in project pages, real range observed: $20 (small social posts) to $10,000 (full SaaS product design) — huge real variance depending on market tier and region.
- "Logo Sell: Available" — speculative, no-client logo work explicitly offered for purchase. Confirmed as a real, recurring business model, not a one-off.
- Real comment sections function as active lead-generation channels — designers reply individually to commenters by name, and at least one real exchange showed a viewer directly requesting collaboration in the comments.

## PART 5.1 — WHAT DRAWS REAL CRITICISM (rare, but present and valuable)

- Missing an introductory/context section before diving straight into visuals — one real comment noted this gap directly on an otherwise-praised project
- Strong visuals paired with generic or weak brand messaging — noted explicitly on a real ukiyo-e-inspired packaging project ("Consider stronger brand messaging")
- Vague, unspecific project descriptions correlate directly with the lowest real engagement observed in the entire sample

## PART 6 — CROSS-PROJECT CONSISTENCY (real precedent for Vitrine's own known gap)

Three separate real studios/agencies were each confirmed maintaining one identical visual signature across every single case study on their own Behance profile, regardless of which client the project was for:
- **Orbix Studio** — identical black title-bar header format across multiple, unrelated client projects
- **Rondesignlab** — consistent studio credit and visual approach across a 16-person team's projects
- **UPROCK** — consistent sub-brand studio identity across multiple mobile app case studies

Additionally, at least one designer explicitly built a connected case-study series — a web dashboard project's own description directly referenced and linked back to an earlier mobile app case study from the same designer, framing the new project as a continuation.

**This is real, direct precedent that professional designers actively want and build a single coherent presence across their published work — not disconnected, independently-styled pieces.** This should raise the priority of eventually building Vitrine's multi-project consistency feature (see PRD.md Known Gaps).

## PART 8 — HONESTY & DISCLOSURE RULES (now with real precedent)

1. **AI-generated content**: list standard AI tools in Tools field, no special treatment. For substantial or sensitive AI use, add a short dedicated disclosure section explaining the human/AI division of labor — real precedent exists and is well-received.
2. **Abandoned/unfinished real client work**: real designers do disclose this honestly (a real olive-oil brand case study disclosed the actual client relationship fell through, and the designer finished the concept independently) — this reads as authentic, not embarrassing. Never fabricate a fake "successful launch" to avoid an honest "this didn't ship" disclosure.
3. **Third-party IP/licensed work**: disclose the actual rights holder and the designer's specific contribution scope, as seen in the Angry Birds/Rovio example.
4. **NDA-protected work**: [carried from v1/v2, still valid] — placeholder client name, plain redaction statement, never a fabricated replacement story.
